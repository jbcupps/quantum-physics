Inside the mathematics & live data

We use a periodic, zero-mean signal with nine possible harmonics. A 512-point discrete Fourier transform measures aₙ = (2/N)Σ fⱼ cos(2πnj/N) and bₙ = (2/N)Σ fⱼ sin(2πnj/N). Orthogonality separates the frequencies. For our sine-plus-phase convention, aₙ = Aₙ sin φₙ and bₙ = Aₙ cos φₙ. Energy is the period average of f², proportional to Σ Aₙ². The dashed curve keeps only frequencies at or below the selected cutoff.

Square and sawtooth presets are truncated Fourier series, not exact discontinuous waves. Amplitudes are dimensionless; the fundamental frequency is 1/T.

02 / FOUR LAWS. ONE CONNECTED WORLD.

The field guide.
Maxwell’s equations.

Charges shape electric fields. Magnetic fields have no isolated sources. Changing fields circulate around one another. Together, these four laws describe classical electromagnetism.

A POINT CHARGE IN SPACE
FLUX THROUGH THE SPHERE112.94 N·m²/C

Field lines point away from a positive charge and toward a negative one. The dashed circle is a section through a spherical Gaussian surface. Every enclosing sphere has the same total electric flux.

The connection: The first two laws constrain flux through closed surfaces. The other two relate circulation around a loop to current and changing flux through its spanning surface.

Geometry, units & model assumptions

The equations here use SI units in vacuum. E is electric field, B magnetic field, Q enclosed charge, I enclosed conduction current, and Φ the flux through a surface. ε₀ and μ₀ are the vacuum constants. A closed-surface integral is marked ∯; a closed-loop integral is marked ∮.

The electric view is a cross-section of an isolated point charge. The magnetic view shows closed field lines around a long straight wire; the rectangular surface section has as much inward as outward magnetic flux. Faraday’s view assumes a prescribed, spatially uniform B(t) within a disk of radius R = 1 m. At its rim, Eθ = −(R/2)dB/dt. Ampère–Maxwell’s view looks along the axis of an ideal circular capacitor gap with uniform E(t); I = 0 in the gap and Bθ = (μ₀ε₀R/2)dE/dt at the rim. Both changing-field examples are local, quasistatic idealizations that neglect edge effects; they are not full radiation solutions. Their physical cycle is 1 second, animated more slowly. Field-line spacing and arrows are illustrative.

This is what
light is doing.

An electric field and a magnetic field oscillate in step, at right angles. Their shared pattern travels through vacuum at the speed of light—a wave solution of Maxwell’s equations.

Look at a crest: Both fields reach their maximum together. E × B points along the direction of energy flow. Changing the wavelength changes both frequency and photon energy.

What the drawing represents

This is a linearly polarized plane wave in vacuum. It shows field values along a line, not a material ribbon moving through space. Cyan is E and coral is cB, so the drawn amplitudes match even though E and B have different units. Time is slowed enormously, and the axes are diagrammatic. k = 2π/λ, ω = 2πf, c = 299,792,458 m/s. The photon readout gives energy per photon, not total beam intensity; this classical field picture does not visualize an individual photon.

04 / LIGHT HAS A DIRECTION

A different angle.
A different outcome.

A polarizer passes the electric-field component along its axis. Rotate the analyzer and watch the projection—and the transmitted intensity—change.

LOOKING INTO THE BEAMIDEAL LINEAR POLARIZER

MALUS’S LAW

I / I₀ = cos² θ
Angle between axes45°
Transmitted intensity50.0%

The quantum connection: For a photon prepared in this linear polarization, cos² θ is also its probability of passing an ideal analyzer. It does not mean that a fraction of one photon emerges.

Why the intensity is squared

The transmitted field amplitude is E₀ cos θ. Intensity is proportional to the time average of the field squared, giving I = I₀ cos² θ. I₀ here is the intensity of an already linearly polarized input beam; the one-half factor for initially unpolarized light is not part of this example. Directions are measured counterclockwise from the horizontal and polarization axes repeat every 180°.

Read the amber curve: |ψ|² is probability density; probabilities come from areas under it. The oscillating cyan curve is the real part of a complex wavefunction, not the particle’s path.

Normalization & the uncertainty principle

These are dimensionless conjugate coordinates x and k with a fixed mean wave number k₀ = 5. The plotted Gaussian densities each integrate to 1. Δ denotes standard deviation, and the shaded bands mark ±1Δ. The real-space density is √(2/π) Δk exp(−2Δk²x²); the complex amplitude is proportional to exp(−Δk²x²) exp(ik₀x). The distributions use independent vertical scales, labeled on each plot. This is a snapshot at t = 0, with no time evolution or measurement collapse simulated.

All states satisfy Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2. This unchirped Gaussian reaches equality. The relation comes from the wavefunction’s Fourier structure, rather than an imperfection in a measuring device.
